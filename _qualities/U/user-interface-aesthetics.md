Definitions:

>
>Derived from [ISO-25010-2011](/references/#iso-25010-2011)

### Lineage in the ISO quality models

The term has been renamed twice. ISO 9126 called it [attractiveness](/qualities/attractiveness); [ISO 25010:2011](/references/#iso-25010-2011) called it *user interface aesthetics*; [ISO 25010:2023](/references/#iso-25010-2023) replaced it with [user engagement](/qualities/user-engagement). Each rename shifted the focus a little further from the *means* (how the interface looks) towards the *objective* (whether people keep using it).

### Not the same as user engagement

This site keeps aesthetics and engagement as separate qualities, because they sit at different points of a cause-and-effect chain:

* **Aesthetics** is a perceptual property: how the interface looks and feels at the moment a user sees it. It is judged before any sustained interaction takes place, and it is measured by looking — with aesthetics scales, expert review, or comparisons of visual variants.
* **Engagement** is a behavioural outcome: whether users keep interacting, return, and recommend the product. It is measured over time — with session length, return rate, or questionnaires such as the User Engagement Scale.

The research literature treats aesthetic appeal as one *contributor* to engagement, alongside focused attention, novelty, felt involvement and perceived usability ([O'Brien & Toms 2008](/references/#obrien2008engagement)). A part cannot be a synonym for the whole. The two can also exist without each other: a beautiful dashboard that nobody opens twice is aesthetic but not engaging; a plain command-line tool that people use daily is engaging but not aesthetic.

Aesthetics also influences how usable a product *appears* to be: users rate attractive interfaces as easier to use even when they are not — the [aesthetic-usability effect](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/) first shown by Kurosu & Kashimura in 1995.
